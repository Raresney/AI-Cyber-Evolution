\title{Does Detection Generalize? A Technique-Generalization Ladder for
Red--Blue Co-Evolution on Real Host Telemetry}

\author{Anonymous}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
Learned and co-evolved intrusion detectors are usually scored on held-out
samples of the \emph{same} attack, which rewards memorizing a tool's artifacts
rather than learning the technique. We ask instead \emph{how far} detection
transfers, and measure it with a five-level \emph{technique-generalization
ladder} grounded in MITRE ATT\&CK and evaluated on real Windows host telemetry
(Open Threat Research Forge Security-Datasets): known $\rightarrow$ new-instance
(unseen tool, same sub-technique) $\rightarrow$ new-subtechnique $\rightarrow$
new-technique $\rightarrow$ new-chain (a full multi-step intrusion). Evaluation
is leakage-free: a detector sees only telemetry, and
evaluator-only ground truth is joined by event id after the decision. On
credential dumping (T1003.001 LSASS) and its neighbours (T1003.003 NTDS, T1055
process injection) we compare a signature detector, a behavioral invariant, and
a local LLM (zero- and few-shot). A hand-coded invariant generalizes perfectly
across \emph{tools} (recall $1.00$) but is \emph{surface-locked}: it scores
$0.00$ across the sub-technique and technique boundaries, where only a separate,
surface-matched rule works. A 3B LLM collapses monotonically down the ladder
($0.71\!\rightarrow\!0.07\!\rightarrow\!0.00$), substituting lexical cues for
technique reasoning. Scaling to 8B overturns the collapse---recall rises to
$1.00/1.00/0.67$ and the model recognizes the injection primitive the 3B model
did not---but at a \emph{precision} cost that deepens with ladder distance
($1.00\!\rightarrow\!0.67\!\rightarrow\!0.13$), and off-target in-context
examples actively hurt. Scaling further to 14B recovers the lost precision at
short distance (new-subtechnique $0.67\!\rightarrow\!0.93$ at full recall) but
only shifts its operating point toward precision at the far rungs---a frontier
capacity moves along, not off. On a full APT29 intrusion ($196$k events) the invariants
transfer with zero false positives yet cover only $\sim\!15\%$ of the chain's
techniques, while a broad LLM recovers coverage at $0.15$ precision---the same
trade at chain scale. Detection generalization is thus not a cliff but a
precision/recall frontier that shifts with model scale and is bounded by the
telemetry surface and the intent within it. Finally, an adaptive adversary that
mutates its tool collapses or oscillates a signature blocklist but cannot move a
surface-bound invariant off $1.00$---the arms race and the ladder are one axis,
mutating within a surface versus crossing one. We release the slices, detectors,
and a stdlib co-evolution engine as a reproducible benchmark.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Red--blue co-evolution and learned detection are an active area, but the field
largely measures the wrong thing. A detector trained on one capture of an attack
and tested on another capture of the \emph{same} attack can score well by keying
on incidental artifacts---a tool's image name, a fixed path---without any grasp
of the underlying technique, exactly the kind of shortcut and sampling bias that
inflates reported security-ML performance~\cite{arp2022,tesseract}. Such a
detector fails silently the moment the adversary swaps tools or sub-techniques,
which is what real adversaries do. Prior work on experimental bias asks whether
a classifier holds up across \emph{time and deployment}~\cite{tesseract}; we ask
the orthogonal question of whether detection holds up across the \emph{attack
taxonomy}---the dimension an adversary controls directly.

We argue the question worth measuring is \emph{generalization distance}: given a
detector that works on a known attack, how far from that attack does its
detection still hold? We make this concrete with a \textbf{technique-%
generalization ladder} whose rungs are defined by MITRE ATT\&CK structure, and
we evaluate it on \emph{real} Windows/Sysmon host telemetry rather than
simulation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A five-level generalization ladder (known, new-instance,
  new-subtechnique, new-technique, new-chain) and a leakage-free evaluation
  protocol for it on real host telemetry (\S\ref{sec:ladder}).
  \item A head-to-head of three detector families---signature, behavioral
  invariant, and a local LLM (zero/few-shot)---across the ladder on credential
  dumping and two neighbouring techniques (\S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A scale ablation (3B, 8B, 14B) showing the small-model ``collapse'' is an

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Adversarial co-evolution.}
A growing line treats attack and defense as co-adapting populations. ACEA~\cite{acea}
is representative: attacker and defender are evolved against each other in the
\emph{LLM jailbreak} domain, where the ``telemetry'' is prompts and responses.
This captures the arms-race dynamic but not host-based intrusion detection, and
it measures escalation, not how far a learned detector transfers to unseen
attacks. The generative half of our setup echoes older attacker-vs-detector
arms races such as MalGAN~\cite{malgan}, where a generator is trained to evade a
surrogate of a black-box malware classifier; and LLM-driven autonomous red
teaming such as PentestGPT~\cite{pentestgpt}, which shows models handle isolated
sub-tasks yet struggle with the whole attack process. Our co-evolution engine
follows the same spirit---a Red that mutates its tool and access to evade a
learning Blue---but we close it onto \emph{real host telemetry} and, crucially,
add an external yardstick those lines lack: the generalization ladder, which
asks not whether the adversary escalates but how far a detector transfers.

\paragraph{Generalization of learned defenses.}
Beyond CAGE~\cite{beyondcage} studies whether reinforcement-learned network-%
defense policies generalize beyond the CybORG scenarios they were trained on.
It shares our central worry---learned defenders may overfit their training
distribution---but operates at the \emph{network} level in simulation and varies
scenarios/topologies rather than position on the ATT\&CK technique hierarchy.
We complement it with a host-telemetry, technique-structured notion of
generalization distance and a detector-agnostic protocol (signature, invariant,
LLM) rather than a single RL policy.

\paragraph{Data and taxonomy.}
We build on OTRF Security-Datasets~\cite{otrf}, openly licensed captures of real
Windows/Sysmon telemetry recorded while executing documented ATT\&CK
procedures; this lets us derive leakage-free slices with per-event ground truth
instead of synthesizing attacks. MITRE ATT\&CK~\cite{attack} supplies the
tactic/technique/sub-technique/procedure hierarchy that \emph{defines} our
rungs: a ``new-instance'' is a sibling procedure, a ``new-subtechnique'' a
sibling sub-technique, a ``new-technique'' a different technique. To our
knowledge, generalization \emph{across} this hierarchy, on real host telemetry
and with a leakage-free protocol, has not been measured; that gap is what the
ladder fills.

\section{The Generalization Ladder}
\label{sec:ladder}

\subsection{Rungs}
We define rungs by distance in the ATT\&CK hierarchy from a detector's training
attack:
\begin{description}
  \item[Known] the trained attack itself.
  \item[New-instance] a different \emph{tool} (procedure) of the same
  sub-technique---e.g.\ a new LSASS dumper.
  \item[New-subtechnique] a different sub-technique of the same technique---e.g.\
  LSASS memory (T1003.001) $\rightarrow$ NTDS database (T1003.003).
  \item[New-technique] a different technique entirely---e.g.\ OS Credential
  Dumping (T1003) $\rightarrow$ Process Injection (T1055).
  \item[New-chain] a full multi-step intrusion rather than an isolated
  technique---many ATT\&CK techniques interleaved in one real host stream.
\end{description}
Each step up changes the observable \emph{surface} and/or the \emph{intent}
within a shared surface; the ladder asks which detectors survive which step.

\subsection{Leakage-free evaluation}
Each raw capture is split into a detector-visible stream (normalized
Windows/Sysmon events, no labels) and an evaluator-only ground-truth stream
joined by an opaque \texttt{event\_index}. A detector emits a per-event decision
from telemetry alone; the evaluator applies labels only afterward. Ground truth
is a \emph{derived} rule per technique (documented, not hand-annotated), chosen
to mark the decisive malicious act and deliberately not to be a complete
detector (Table~\ref{tab:gt}).

\subsection{Detectors}
\begin{description}
  \item[Signature] learns malicious \emph{tool image names} from training
  attacks; fires on a known-bad image. The realistic IOC baseline.
  \item[Behavioral invariant] encodes the technique's observable invariant
  (e.g.\ a non-system process reading \texttt{lsass.exe} with a memory-read
  mask). Surface-specific by construction; a \emph{separate} invariant exists
  per surface.
  \item[LLM] a local model (Ollama) judges each event, zero-shot and few-shot.
  The prompt is scoped to the rung: technique-specific for within-technique
  rungs, general ``any malicious technique'' for the cross-technique rung.
\end{description}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.}
We evaluate on four leakage-free slices derived from the Open Threat Research
Forge (OTRF) Security-Datasets~\cite{otrf} (MIT-licensed), each a real
Windows/Sysmon capture recorded while executing a documented ATT\&CK procedure.
The LSASS slice spans seven procedures and $354{,}229$ events, split
\emph{by procedure} into a three-procedure training set and a four-procedure
holdout, so that every holdout tool is genuinely unseen at training time. The
NTDS slice ($3$ procedures, $41{,}996$ events) and the injection slice
($3$ procedures, $14{,}218$ events) each supply a neighbouring (sub-)technique
for the cross-boundary rungs. The chain slice is MITRE ATT\&CK Evaluations APT29
Day~1 ($196{,}081$ events), labeled only on the three techniques whose
ground-truth rule we can derive ($5$ attack events). All slices are regenerated
from the raw captures by the released loaders, which enforce the leakage-free
split.

\paragraph{Models.}
The LLM detector uses two local models served by Ollama~\cite{ollama}:
\texttt{llama3.2} (3B) and \texttt{llama3.1:8b}. Both run deterministically
(temperature $0$, a fixed seed, and a capped output length), so every reported
LLM number is reproducible rather than a sample from a stochastic decoder. Each
detector---signature, invariant, or LLM---emits one Boolean decision per event
from telemetry alone.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report recall over \emph{all} labeled attack events and precision (with the
false-positive rate where it clarifies) against a seeded benign subsample drawn
from the same event surface as the attacks. A metric is left \emph{undefined}
rather than set to $0$ when its denominator is zero---a transfer detector that
never fires has no precision to report. Every value in the tables below is the
measured output in the released \texttt{results/*.json}.

\paragraph{Invariants are surface-locked.}
The behavioral invariant is perfect across tools (Table~\ref{tab:rung1}) yet
$0.00$ across both the sub-technique and technique boundaries
(Tables~\ref{tab:rung2},~\ref{tab:rung3}); only a separate surface-matched rule
recovers detection. On injection the LSASS invariant shares the very EID10
events it inspects but, keying on \texttt{VM\_READ}-to-\texttt{lsass.exe},
neither fires nor \emph{misfires} ($0$ FP): generalization is intent-local
within a surface.

\paragraph{Signature memorization has two tells.}
The signature detector learns the malicious source images in training
(\texttt{procdump.exe}, \texttt{procdump64.exe}, \texttt{rundll32.exe},
\texttt{winx64\_payload.exe}). This is not even clean on the training split:
\texttt{rundll32.exe} is a living-off-the-land binary that also touches
\texttt{lsass.exe} benignly, so learning it as an IOC drops training precision to
$0.50$ (six false positives). On the holdout its recall is $0.29$, and only
because one held-out procedure (\texttt{campaign\_04}) reuses a tool image seen
in training; the three genuinely unseen dumpers (sharpdump, dumpert, nanodump)
score $0.00$. Keying on tool identity thus fails both ways---it does not
generalize to unseen tools and it is not even precise on seen ones.

\paragraph{Small LLM: lexical, not technical.}
The 3B model degrades $0.71\!\rightarrow\!0.07\!\rightarrow\!0.00$. Its only
NTDS catch is the event literally named \texttt{ntds.dit}; it judged a textbook
\texttt{CreateRemoteThread} into \texttt{notepad.exe} ``normal process
execution.'' It substitutes surface strings for technique reasoning.

Scaling 3B $\rightarrow$ 8B overturns the collapse (Table~\ref{tab:scale}):
recall rises at every rung and 8B recognizes the injection primitive 3B missed.
The cost moves to \emph{precision}, which falls with ladder distance
($1.00\!\rightarrow\!0.67\!\rightarrow\!0.13$): 8B transfers by becoming a broad,
trigger-happy detector that flags ordinary process-access handles
(\texttt{svchost}$\rightarrow$\texttt{svchost}) to catch the real attacks.
Few-shot is \emph{directional}: same-family exemplars raise NTDS precision
($0.26\!\rightarrow\!0.67$) while holding recall, but off-target T1003 exemplars
\emph{destroy} injection recall ($0.67\!\rightarrow\!0.00$) by anchoring the
model to credential dumping. Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} summarizes the 3B/8B
trends: recall rising with scale, precision falling with ladder distance.

Scaling again to 14B answers the question this trade raises---whether capacity
\emph{recovers} the lost precision or merely moves along the frontier---and the
answer depends on distance. At the near rungs it recovers it: 14B holds recall
at $1.00$ while lifting new-subtechnique precision $0.67\!\rightarrow\!0.93$, a
strict Pareto gain over 8B. At the far rung it does not: 14B's new-technique
operating point trades recall for precision
($0.67/0.13\!\rightarrow\!0.33/0.50$) rather than breaking through. Capacity
thus shifts the trade toward precision and, at short distance, dissolves it, but
the far-rung wall persists---it moves the model along the precision/recall
frontier, one rung further out, not off it.

\section{New-Chain Rung}
\label{sec:chain}
The atomic rungs isolate one technique at a time; a real intrusion interleaves
many. We run the \emph{unchanged} per-technique detectors over MITRE ATT\&CK
Evaluations APT29 Day~1 (OTRF \texttt{compound/apt29}), a multi-step Windows
intrusion of $196{,}081$ host events from a different environment, labeling only
the three techniques we can derive (an undercount of true malice, stated as a
limitation). This answers two questions a per-technique benchmark cannot.

attack events over $3$ techniques; NTDS absent in Day~1).}
\label{tab:chain}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Detector & Recall & Precision & Flagged \\
\midrule
invariant union (3 techniques)  & \textbf{1.00} & \textbf{1.00} & 5 / 196k \\
LLM 8B (general, same surface)  & 0.80 & 0.15 & 27 / 55 \\
LLM 14B (general, same surface) & 0.60 & \textbf{1.00} & 3 / 55 \\
\bottomrule

\paragraph{Transfer with a realistic false-alarm budget.}
The invariants were derived on small atomic captures; here they meet $196$k
mixed events from a different host. Each still fires on its technique's instances
with \emph{zero} false positives over the full stream (LSASS $3/3$, injection
$2/2$; NTDS does not occur in Day~1), for a union recall of $1.00$ at precision
$1.00$ and $0$ FP in $196{,}076$ benign events (Table~\ref{tab:chain}).
Surface-locked detection transfers across environments intact---the atomic
slices could not measure this false-alarm rate at scale, and it holds. Run over
every processed dataset at once---$606{,}524$ real events from four capture
environments---the three invariants raise a combined \emph{three} false
positives (rate $\approx 5\times10^{-6}$): the LSASS and NTDS rules exactly zero,
the injection rule three benign \texttt{CreateRemoteThread}s in the LSASS
captures. Every off-diagonal is silent (recall $0.00$, near-zero FP), so the
rules do not interfere---which is what makes them safe to stack
(\S\ref{sec:layering}).

\paragraph{Coverage versus precision.}
But the detector set has rules for only three of the $\sim\!20$ techniques Day~1
exercises ($\sim\!15\%$): precise per-technique sensors illuminate a few steps of
the kill chain and are dark for the rest---the deterministic end of a
coverage/precision trade. The complementary 8B LLM takes the other end. Running
the general ``any malicious technique?'' detector over the same handle/thread
surface ($55$ events: the $5$ attacks plus a $50$-event seeded benign subsample)
recovers broader intent---recall $0.80$---but flags $27/55$ events
($\sim\!49\%$) at precision $0.15$, drowning the $5$ real events in $23$ false
alarms, overwhelmingly ordinary \texttt{svchost}$\rightarrow$\texttt{svchost}
handles. Scaling that same general detector to 14B converts it from
trigger-happy to precise---it flags only $3/55$ events, all three real, for
precision $1.00$ at recall $0.60$---but buys that precision by surrendering the
recall that defined 8B's breadth, missing two of the five chain steps. At chain
scale the axis is explicit: narrow rules buy precision and lose coverage; a broad
LLM buys coverage and loses precision, and scaling it slides along the same axis
toward precision rather than off it. None reads the chain.

\section{Adaptive Adversary}
\label{sec:adversary}
Rungs 1--4 hold the attacker fixed and move the test. The obvious objection is
that the invariants win only because the adversary never adapts. This rung lets
Red adapt: it \emph{mutates its tool} to evade the defender, played out over the
real LSASS slice (the $7$ campaigns are $7$ real credential-dumping tools). The
defender runs two detectors side by side---a signature IOC blocklist of
malicious image names, seeded with the one tool the SOC already blocks, and the
behavioral invariant. When adaptive, the signature learns the current tool's
image post-incident (always one round behind); the invariant never learns.
Adaptive Red, when its current tool is caught, \emph{mints}: it switches to a
real slice tool whose image the signature has not yet learned.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Arms race on the real LSASS slice ($12$ rounds). Red adaptive $\times$
signature-Blue adaptive; the behavioral invariant never adapts.}
\label{tab:adversary}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llc@{}}
\toprule
Cond.\ (Red / sig-Blue) & signature recall & invariant \\
\midrule
A static / static    & $1.00$ flat                 & \textbf{$1.00$ flat} \\
B adaptive / static  & $1.00\!\rightarrow\!0.00$   & \textbf{$1.00$ flat} \\
C static / adaptive  & $1.00$ flat                 & \textbf{$1.00$ flat} \\
D adaptive / adaptive & $1\,0\,1\,0\ldots$ (osc.)  & \textbf{$1.00$ flat} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:adversary} is the whole story. Against an adaptive Red a static
blocklist collapses permanently (B: recall $\rightarrow 0.00$ after one escape)
and an adaptive blocklist only \emph{oscillates} (D: $1\,0\,1\,0$, Red one unseen
tool ahead of what forensics taught Blue last round), while bloating into
false positives as it learns shared LOLBin names (precision $0.25$ then $0.50$).
That finite arms race terminates only because these seven real tools share image
names, so Red exhausts its disjoint-image escapes; an unbounded adversary that
simply \emph{renames} the payload each round (the access mask untouched) never
terminates---the blocklist grows without bound and the signature recall
oscillates forever, while the invariant stays $1.00$. The dynamics are
seed-invariant: condition D seeded from each of the seven tools gives the same
mean recall ($0.67$), oscillation swing ($0.73$), and flat invariant.
The invariant holds $1.00/1.00$ in \emph{every} round of \emph{every}
condition and learns nothing: a tool swap does not change the
EID10 VM\_READ$\rightarrow$\texttt{lsass} surface, so there is nothing to evade.
Adaptation is not what wins---being bound to the technique's \emph{surface}
rather than its tool is. Red's only escape from the invariant is to leave that
surface, i.e.\ change the technique---which is exactly the ladder move of
rungs~2--3, and exactly where the invariant also fails. Cheap adaptation (mutate
the tool within a surface) is the arms race; expensive adaptation (change the
technique across a surface) is the ladder: one axis from two ends. The blocklist
hardened by the entire arms race, tested across the boundary on NTDS, scores
$0.00$---within-surface hardening transfers nothing down a rung.

\section{Layered Detection}
\label{sec:layering}
The rungs map where each family fails; the defender's question is constructive.
If the precise surface invariant is \emph{stacked} under the broad LLM, what
operating point results at each ladder distance? We answer it offline from the
saved $14$B verdicts: on each rung's judged events we recompute the decision of
the invariants a defender has \emph{already built for known techniques}---never
a rule for the rung's own novel technique. The LSASS rule applied to an NTDS or
injection event does not fire, so this reproduces the ladder's $0.00$ transfer
rather than a surface-matched oracle. The invariant is the trusted \emph{block}
tier (auto-actionable); the LLM judges only invariant-negatives as a
\emph{triage} tier.

\emph{is} the LLM: it inherits the LLM's recall and its false positives, with no
high-confidence tier to fall back on until someone writes the new surface rule.
The union never exceeds the better of its members at any rung---stacking does
not beat the frontier. Its entire value is the free, auto-actionable block tier,
and that tier's reach is exactly the defender's accumulated surface rules:
ladder distance decides whether you get a $0$-FP block tier or noisy LLM triage.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Read across the ladder, no detector family is general; each fails in a
characteristic way, and the two failures are complementary. The behavioral
invariant is a precise, narrow sensor: perfect within its surface and
silent---not merely wrong---outside it, which is what makes it safe to stack but
useless as a sole defense. Covering the ATT\&CK hierarchy with invariants means
writing one per surface, and the chain rung shows the ceiling plainly: three
hand-built rules illuminate about $15\%$ of a real intrusion, but do so with zero
false alarms across $196$k events. The LLM is the mirror image. At 3B it is
lexical---it reads tool names and literal strings rather than behavior; at 8B it
recovers behavioral recall but pays in precision that decays with ladder
distance, transferring only by becoming trigger-happy. On the full chain that
same breadth buries five real events under twenty-three false alarms. Scaling on
to 14B converts that breadth back into precision at short distance (a Pareto gain
over 8B) but, at the far rungs and on the chain, only trades recall for precision
rather than winning both. Scale thus moves the model along a precision/recall
frontier rather than off a cliff, and in-context examples steer it
\emph{directionally}: helpful when on-surface, actively harmful when off-surface.

For a defender the practical reading is that these are two ends of one trade, not
two candidates for one slot. Narrow invariants give high-confidence, low-noise
coverage of the few techniques they encode and belong at the anchor of an
alerting pipeline; a broad model widens coverage but needs a human analyst or a
second stage to survive its false-positive rate. The contribution is the
\emph{map} of where each family sits, and the ladder is the coordinate system
that turns ``does it generalize?'' into a measurable distance rather than an
anecdote.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Construct validity.} Ground truth is a \emph{derived} per-technique rule
marking the decisive malicious act, not an exhaustive hand annotation; it can
miss precursor or cleanup steps, and on the chain it labels only three
techniques. The rules are deliberately conservative, so this biases coverage
\emph{downward} (an undercount of malice), never upward. \textbf{Statistical
validity.} Attack-event counts per rung are small ($7/14/3$ atomic, $5$ on the
chain) because real captures of a single isolated technique are sparse; recall is
therefore coarse-grained, and we report it beside the raw TP/FN counts rather
than as a smooth rate. LLM precision is measured on seeded benign subsamples of
the attack surface, not the full stream; the invariants' false-alarm rate, by
contrast, is measured on the full $606{,}524$-event stream across all four
environments (\S\ref{sec:chain}). \textbf{External validity.} The models
are 3B/8B/14B local models, not frontier or tool-calling systems, and the telemetry
is Windows/Sysmon from a single dataset family. We therefore claim the
\emph{shape} of the result---invariants surface-lock, small LLMs read lexically,
and scale trades recall for precision with distance---rather than the exact
numerical values, which another model or dataset would shift. The leakage-free
protocol and the released slices exist precisely so that this shape can be
retested elsewhere.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We recast detection generalization as \emph{distance} on an ATT\&CK ladder and
measured it on real host telemetry under a leakage-free protocol. Detection is
bounded by the telemetry surface and the intent within it: hand-built invariants
are surface-locked, and an LLM's transfer is a scale-dependent precision/recall
frontier that turns into a coverage/precision trade once the target is a full
intrusion rather than one technique. Three directions follow directly. Our 14B
ablation already shows the pattern: more capacity recovers the precision 8B loses
at \emph{short} distance but only slides along the frontier at the far rungs, so
a true frontier or tool-calling model would test whether that pattern holds or
finally breaks the far-rung wall. Denser
chain labeling, beyond the three techniques we can derive today, would sharpen
the coverage measurement. And the adaptive-adversary rung
(\S\ref{sec:adversary}) already closes the engine loop on this slice---an
arms race Red cannot win against a surface-bound invariant; a learned Red that
synthesizes novel tooling, rather than selecting from a finite real pool, is the
natural next adversary. The thread tying all of it together is a single axis:
mutating within a telemetry surface is the arms race, crossing one is the
ladder, and detection generalizes exactly as far as a detector reads intent
rather than tool.
